Questions for the Director — Demo Reference Answers

Direct answers referencing specific charts, dates, months, and numbers from the 2025 dataset.

1. Seasonality (Tasks 2, 6, 7) Worst months: February (mean 141.9 µg/m³), December (138.8 µg/m³), January (137.6 µg/m³), and November (129.0 µg/m³). Best months: July (mean 27.1 µg/m³), June (32.7 µg/m³), and August (36.9 µg/m³). The worst month (February, 141.9 µg/m³) is 5.2 times higher than the best month (July, 27.1 µg/m³).
2. Anomalies (Task 2 & Task 7) The two highest days are 2025-11-08 (320.0 µg/m³) and 2025-05-18 (245.0 µg/m³). Both are isolated single-day spikes jumping far above surrounding days (especially May 18, which occurred during a moderate spring month averaging 59.9 µg/m³). Real-world causes include intense crop-residue burning + stagnant thermal inversion in November, and a pre-monsoon dust storm or nearby biomass/industrial fire on May 18.
3. Guideline Exceedances (Task 2) 355 out of 356 recorded days (99.7%) exceeded the WHO 24-hour guideline of 15 µg/m³ (only 2025-07-09 at 11.8 µg/m³ was safe). 188 out of 356 recorded days (52.8%) exceeded the WHO Interim Target 1 of 75 µg/m³.
4. Build-up of Unhealthy Days (Task 3) Unhealthy days (> 75 µg/m³) build up fastest in winter and late autumn: January (31/31 days), February (28/28 days), March (21/22 days), November (30/30 days), and December (31/31 days), where the slope rises by 1 day every day. The curve goes completely flat from June to August (0 days above 75 µg/m³ in Jun, Jul, Aug; and only 2 days in May).
5. City vs. Suburb Comparison (Task 4) Yes, the suburb follows the same U-shaped seasonal pattern and experiences the same spike dates (Nov 8 at 290.0 µg/m³, May 18 at 210.0 µg/m³), but stays ~20% cleaner overall. They differ most in winter — especially February (mean gap of 32.7 µg/m³: City 141.9 vs. Suburb 109.2 µg/m³) and January (27.4 µg/m³) — because winter temperature inversions trap urban traffic and local emissions in the city centre, whereas in July they differ by only 5.2 µg/m³.
6. Monthly Composition & Missing Days (Task 5) Months with Severe (> 150 µg/m³) days: December (11 days), January (9), February (8), November (5), October (2), March (1), and May (1). Month with the most Low (≤ 35 µg/m³) days: July (28 days), followed by June (17) and August (12). March is shorter (22 days) because 9 days of readings (2025-03-12 to 2025-03-20) are missing while the sensor was offline.
7. Day-of-Week vs. Monthly Pattern (Task 6 Heatmap) The pattern is overwhelmingly by month (season) rather than day of the week. Across weekdays, the annual average barely varies (from 80.6 µg/m³ on Saturday to 90.4 µg/m³ on Tuesday), whereas monthly averages swing from 27.1 µg/m³ (Jul) to 141.9 µg/m³ (Feb). This shows pollution is primarily driven by seasonal meteorology (winter inversions vs. summer monsoon washout) and regional winter sources rather than weekday commuter traffic alone.
8. Monthly Spread (Task 7 Band Chart) The gap between best and worst day is widest in November with a spread of 239.1 µg/m³ (min 80.9 µg/m³ on Nov 2 to max 320.0 µg/m³ on Nov 8), followed by May (spread 213.6 µg/m³: min 31.4 to max 245.0 µg/m³). A monthly average hides this by smoothing extreme spikes into a single number — for example, May's mean of 59.9 µg/m³ looks moderate and hides the hazardous 245.0 µg/m³ emergency on May 18.
9. Trend & Data Quality (Task 8) There is no one-way long-term rise or fall across 2025 — only a strong seasonal cycle (starting around ~138 µg/m³ in Jan, dropping to ~27 µg/m³ in Jul, and returning to ~139 µg/m³ in Dec). Exactly 9 days are missing (2025-03-12 to 2025-03-20). Drawing an unbroken line across the gap is misleading because it fabricates a smooth linear change between March 11 (162.9 µg/m³) and March 21 (144.0 µg/m³), hiding the sensor failure.
10. Zooming into November (Task 9 Brush & Zoom) Zooming into November reveals the day-by-day build-up and recovery around the biggest spike: PM2.5 rose steadily from 80.9 µg/m³ (Nov 2) to 155.9 µg/m³ (Nov 7), spiked to 320.0 µg/m³ on Nov 8, dropped sharply back to 125.7 µg/m³ on Nov 9, and had a smaller secondary peak on Nov 20 (175.5 µg/m³) — details that are squashed together in the full-year view.
11. Why the Committee Member's Pie Chart Failed (1) No time order or rate of change: A pie chart turns monthly averages into slices of a whole, hiding chronological progression, seasonal slopes, and when pollution rises or falls. (2) Hides daily extremes, thresholds, and missing data: It conceals hazardous daily spikes (320.0 µg/m³ on Nov 8), the number of days exceeding 75 µg/m³ (188 days), and the 9-day sensor outage in March.
12. Two-Month Recommendation for School Outdoor Restrictions Recommendation: December and February (or January & December).
• Evidence 1 — Task 5 (Stacked Level Composition): December has the highest number of Severe (>150 µg/m³) days (11 Severe + 20 High = 31/31 days > 75), followed by January (9 Severe + 22 High = 31/31 days > 75) and February (8 Severe + 20 High = 28/28 days > 75).
• Evidence 2 — Task 6 & Task 7 (Heatmap & Monthly Mean/Band): February (141.9 µg/m³) and December (138.8 µg/m³) (with January at 137.6 µg/m³) have the highest monthly averages across all days of the week, meaning children face hazardous air on 100% of school days in those months.
